## Intro

Compiled from questions Anna Kaley fields as instructor of NN/g's "Writing
Compelling Digital Copy" course — what to write, how to structure it, and how to
support the user experience. Grouped by theme. Framing observation: practitioners
increasingly want nuanced, experience-based thinking from professionals rather than
relying on AI answers alone.

## Plain Language and Readability

- **Reading level?** Aim for 6th–8th grade for general audiences. Eyetracking shows
  users read only **20–28% of the text on a page**. Lower reading levels help all
  users process faster with less effort.
- **Does a low reading level exclude educated audiences?** No — "Even experts are
  busy, distracted, and scanning on screens." Plain language benefits all literacy
  levels, including highly educated users.
- **If users read only ~20%, does shortening make them read less?** No — concise,
  well-structured writing increases the *proportion* of content users actually read.
- **Does plain language help neurodiverse users (dyslexia, ADHD)?** Yes — chunked
  content, descriptive subheadings, and bulleted lists help across literacy levels.

## Tone of Voice

- **Voice vs. tone?** Voice = organizational personality (consistent); tone = how
  that voice adapts contextually.
- **Should tone vary by channel/context, and be in the style guide?** Yes — document
  both stable voice attributes and tone-shift guidance.
- **Can AI detect tone? Good enough?** AI can approximate tone but can't predict how
  it lands with specific users in context; usability testing remains best.

## Jargon, Acronyms, and Technical Language

- **When is jargon OK?** Only when research/surveys/site-search logs confirm the
  audience understands and prefers it. For mixed audiences, lead with plain language,
  jargon in parentheses.
- **Spell acronyms out only on first use?** No — digital readers scan and enter pages
  mid-way, missing first-use definitions. Spell out repeatedly unless universal
  (PDF, URL, FAQ).
- **Are branded terms jargon?** Yes — branded names lack self-explanation; descriptive
  labels ("Patient Records & Insurance") communicate purpose immediately.

## Structure, Links, and Calls to Action

- **Literal or clever headings?** Literal, informative ("How We Reduce Processing Time
  by 40%" beats "Our Approach").
- **Use "Learn More" as a link label?** No — uninformative and an accessibility
  failure (screen readers announce it repeatedly with no distinction). Use descriptive
  link text.
- **Include "successfully" in confirmation messages?** Usually not — redundant; let
  design elements (green checkmarks) convey success.

## AI, SEO, Stakeholders, and Copy Testing

- **Use AI to write UX copy?** Yes, strategically — good for first drafts, headlines,
  alt text, brainstorming. Risks: generic output, brand-voice drift, no user/context
  knowledge. Treat AI as "a capable first-draft collaborator" needing review.
- **What metrics in the AI era?** Shift from **traffic** to **engagement, conversion,
  task-completion, and return visits**, since AI tools intercept searches before users
  click through.
- **Does SEO still matter vs. AEO/GEO?** Yes. AEO structures content for AI assistants;
  GEO makes content authoritative for AI citation. Underlying principles unchanged:
  "clear structure, plain language, descriptive headings, direct answers near the top."
  **"Write for people first. Structure it for machines second."**
- **Pushing back when stakeholders prioritize SEO over users?** Good UX writing and SEO
  align (descriptive headings, clear links, plain language serve both). Show qualitative
  research findings.
- **Convincing stakeholders copy matters?** Show evidence — eye-tracking videos,
  usability-test clips, before/after readability scores framed around business outcomes.
- **Affordable alternatives to eyetracking?** Usability testing, first-click testing,
  cloze tests, content-preference tests; heatmap tools (e.g. Hotjar) as rough proxies.

## Conclusion

Organizational constraints (legal, stakeholder preferences, assumptions about reading
behavior) usually underlie UX-writing challenges; data-backed recommendations, user
testing, and focus on actual reading patterns lead to better outcomes.
